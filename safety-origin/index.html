Safety Origins

For years I asked how a car could fail.
Now I ask the same of AI.

As a functional safety engineer, I helped decide when a vehicle system was safe enough to release. When I started to work with AI, I found that many of the same questions still apply. This page tells how that experience shaped the governance in this workbench, one chapter at a time.

Chapter 1

Where I come from

Seven years in automotive functional safety, from analysis work on embedded software to safety governance for complete vehicle systems.

  1. 2023 — PRESENT

    Bosch Engineering

    Functional Safety Manager. ASIL D safety governance for five vehicle systems, safety cases, assessments, tool qualification and technical leadership.

  2. 2019 — 2022

    techcos

    Functional Safety Engineer. Safety analysis coordination for seven embedded software components for BMW, architecture verification, toolchains and release automation.

Four habits came with me.

  • Ask how a system can fail before you trust it.
  • Prefer checks that give the same answer twice.
  • Let someone challenge a result before you accept it.
  • Tie every claim to the evidence behind it.

Chapter 2

Where the analogy stops

A vehicle control system fails in ways that physics and testing can bound. A language model fails in other ways, so the habits need new controls.

Probabilistic outputs

LLM responses can change across runs; a controlled graph does not turn model content into deterministic facts.

Semantic and evidential uncertainty

Claims can be plausible but ungrounded, and reference data can be missing, stale or ambiguous.

Adversarial input and authority

Untrusted documents may contain instructions, while models must never acquire approval rights.

Ongoing change

Model providers, prompts, data and usage patterns require monitoring, drift testing and controlled updates.

Chapter 3

Six decisions that shaped GovernanceAsCode

Each decision starts from one lesson of safety work. Open a decision to read the lesson, what I built from it, and where you can see it in the workbench.

  1. 01Keep deterministic verification behind CoVe reviewsCloseOpen

    What safety work taught me

    My functional safety work taught me to separate uncertain behaviour from checks whose results can be reproduced.

    What I built from it

    I added CoVe reviews to keep AI-style proposals behind an evidence-verification boundary. In this reference workflow, deterministic checks can reject unsupported findings even if a proposal is wrong; approval still requires the human gate.

    Look inside the committee
  2. 02Challenge hallucinations with Proponent and OpponentCloseOpen

    What safety work taught me

    Architecture and software verification taught me to challenge a proposed result before accepting it.

    What I built from it

    I added Proponent and Opponent roles to surface contradictions and challenge hallucinated or unsupported claims. My aim was to improve review quality and confidence in the evidence.

    Replay a case through the committee
  3. 03Use FMEA to expose failure modes and edge casesCloseOpen

    What safety work taught me

    I performed a Failure Mode and Effects Analysis (FMEA) to examine how this system could fail, the effects of each failure and the controls needed.

    What I built from it

    That shaped the failure and edge cases: fabricated findings, missing evidence, incorrect rates and unresolved reviews. The published hazard analysis connects failures to controls, tests and remaining limitations.

    The hazard log
  4. 04Make the system card and process audit traceableCloseOpen

    What safety work taught me

    Owning safety cases and supporting assessments taught me to connect each claim to its controls and evidence.

    What I built from it

    I added the system card and process audit aspects to make scope, limitations and decisions inspectable. Like monitoring software, the event record and replay dashboard make checks, exceptions and state changes visible and traceable.

    The §9 trail
  5. 05Connect lessons learned with incident responseCloseOpen

    What safety work taught me

    My experience with release processes and controlled changes shaped how I think about learning from failures.

    What I built from it

    I included lessons learned and incident response in the governance design so failures can lead to reviewed improvements. A reviewer approves each lesson before it changes a control.

    The lessons ledger
  6. 06Keep human authority and automation limits explicitCloseOpen

    What safety work taught me

    I lead safety governance through to release recommendation, qualify development tools and coordinate technical project teams.

    What I built from it

    That shaped the named reviewer gate, bounded proposals and failure handling. Deterministic checks support the decision; neither an AI-style role nor monitoring software receives final approval authority.

    Validation workspace
